By establishing this secure diagnostic tool, Project Sumpay aims to
restore clinical safety, explicitly ensure bodily autonomy, and advance
health equity for the Deaf female community. To realize this aim, the
study is guided by the following specific objectives:

  1. To identify and analyze the systemic clinical communication barriers
     experienced by Deaf Filipino women in OB-GYN and Gender and
     Development (GAD) office settings through structured qualitative
     interviews with community respondents and systematic literature
     analysis.

  2. To design and develop SUMPAY — a dual-interface, bidirectional,
     and fully offline clinical communication application deployable on
     standard tablet hardware, integrating ASL recognition, speech
     transcription, and text-to-ASL video playback in a single cohesive
     system.

  3. To implement a real-time American Sign Language (ASL) recognition
     pipeline leveraging Google MediaPipe for multi-modal landmark
     extraction from hands and facial expressions simultaneously, and a
     Transformer-based neural network classifier capable of operating
     within the constraints of clinical edge devices.

  4. To implement a secure, fully offline Speech-to-Text (STT) subsystem
     for the physician interface that transcribes spoken clinical
     instructions in real time and triggers contextually appropriate
     pre-recorded ASL video responses for the patient.

  5. To evaluate the usability and clinical accessibility of the
     developed prototype through structured alpha testing and System
     Usability Scale (SUS) assessment involving Deaf female community
     members and healthcare providers.

METHODOLOGY

The development of SUMPAY followed a five-objective sequential research
design addressing problem identification, system architecture, ASL
pipeline implementation, STT pipeline implementation, and usability
evaluation.


Methodology for Objective 1: Identification and Analysis of
Communication Barriers

A preliminary qualitative needs assessment was conducted to empirically
ground the system design in the lived experiences of the target
population. Six (6) Deaf female members of the SMC-Iligan Deaf
Community Organization were recruited through purposive sampling.
Semi-structured interview guides were structured around four thematic
domains: (1) current communication modalities during clinical
consultations; (2) privacy perceptions when family members serve as
interpreters; (3) comprehension difficulties with physician-written
notes; and (4) openness toward tablet-based assistive technology.

Responses were transcribed and subjected to thematic analysis following
Braun and Clarke's six-phase coding framework. Themes were triangulated
with findings from the literature review spanning 21 peer-reviewed
sources in healthcare communication, sign language recognition,

Methodology for Objective 2: System Design and Development

SUMPAY was designed as a dual-interface kiosk application separating
the physician workflow from the patient input modality, consistent with
the role-differentiated display design rationale supported by Ashrafi
et al. [19]. The system encompasses two principal modules:

  Patient Interface (ASL-to-Text): A camera-first input layer where
  the patient's hand gestures and facial expressions are captured,
  processed through Google MediaPipe landmark extraction, and classified
  by a Transformer-based neural network in real time.

  Physician Interface (Speech-to-Text + ASL Playback): A dual-path
  output layer where the physician speaks (captured via Vosk STT) or
  types a message, which is matched to a pre-recorded ASL video clip
  displayed to the patient.

The application was developed in Python using the PyQt6 framework,
structured as a QStackedWidget nine-screen state machine (Figure 1).
Zero external network calls are made at runtime, satisfying full
offline data privacy requirements consistent with RA 10173 and
institutional data governance standards.

Methodology for Objective 3: ASL Recognition Pipeline Implementation

The patient-side recognition pipeline implements a five-stage
sequential architecture from raw camera frame capture to debounced
phrase output.

Stage 1 — Camera Capture (CameraWorker)
A dedicated QThread worker captures frames at 1280x720 resolution and
30 frames per second from the device camera using OpenCV. Each frame is
emitted as a raw NumPy array for downstream processing.

Stage 2 — Multi-Modal Landmark Extraction (MediaPipe)
Each frame is processed by VisionProcessor, which runs two MediaPipe
landmarker tasks in parallel:
  - hand_landmarker.task: extracts 21 three-dimensional (x, y, z)
    spatial landmarks per detected hand (up to two hands), capturing
    finger joint positions, relative angles, and wrist orientation.
  - face_landmarker.task: extracts 478 facial landmarks and 52
    facial action unit blendshape scores, capturing non-manual markers
    — raised eyebrows, cheek puffing, mouth shape — that are
    syntactically significant components of ASL grammar [2].

Stage 3 — Feature Vector Construction (features.py)
Landmark dictionaries are converted into fixed-length per-frame feature
vectors of 178 dimensions (Table 3). Hand landmarks undergo two
normalization steps: (1) translational normalization — the wrist
landmark (index 0) is subtracted making all coordinates wrist-relative;
(2) scale normalization — coordinates are divided by the maximum
absolute value, yielding a unit-scale representation invariant to hand
size, signer body proportions, and camera distance. Absent hands or
failed face landmark detection are zero-padded.

Table 3. Per-Frame Feature Vector Layout (178 Dimensions)
+---------------+------------+------------------------+-------------------------------+
| Segment       | Dimensions | Source                 | Description                   |
+---------------+------------+------------------------+-------------------------------+
| hand_0        | 63 (21x3)  | Hand Landmarker        | Dominant hand — wrist-norm.,  |
|               |            |                        | unit-scaled                   |
| hand_1        | 63 (21x3)  | Hand Landmarker        | Non-dominant hand — zero-pad  |
|               |            |                        | if absent                     |
| blendshapes   | 52         | Face Landmarker        | Facial action unit scores     |
|               |            |                        | (0.0–1.0), zero-pad if absent |
| TOTAL         | 178        |                        |                               |
+---------------+------------+------------------------+-------------------------------+

[FIGURE 2 — Feature Vector Composition Diagram.

Stage 4 — Temporal Windowing and Classification (RecognizerWorker)
A dedicated QThread worker accumulates per-frame feature vectors in a
rolling deque of 60 frames (2.0 seconds at 30 FPS). Every 10 frames
(window stride, ~0.33 s), the full window — shaped (60, 178) — is
passed to the ASLTransformer neural classifier.

The ASLTransformer is a lightweight Transformer encoder adapted for
temporal gesture-sequence classification. Its architecture (Table 4)
projects each frame's 178-dimensional feature vector into a 128-
dimensional latent space, applies sinusoidal positional encoding to
preserve temporal ordering, processes the sequence through 3 stacked
Transformer encoder layers with 4 attention heads each, mean-pools
across the time axis to produce a fixed-length sequence representation,
and outputs class logits via a linear classification head.

Table 4. ASLTransformer Architecture Summary
+----------------------------+--------------------------------------------------+
| Component                  | Configuration                                    |
+----------------------------+--------------------------------------------------+
| Input Projection           | Linear(178 -> 128)                               |
| Positional Encoding        | Sinusoidal, max_len = 512                        |
| Transformer Encoder        | 3 layers, 4 attention heads per layer            |
| Feed-Forward Dim           | 512 (4 x d_model)                                |
| Dropout                    | 0.2                                              |
| Classification Head        | Mean Pool over time -> Linear(128 -> N_classes)  |
| Deployment Format          | TorchScript (.pt), CPU-only                      |
+----------------------------+--------------------------------------------------+

[FIGURE 3 — ASLTransformer Architecture Diagram.
 Show left-to-right flow: Input window (60x178) -> Linear Projection
 (60x128) -> Positional Encoding (60x128) -> Transformer Encoder Layer
 x3 (60x128 each) -> Mean Pool (128) -> Linear + Softmax -> class
 probabilities. Label each block with its shape.]

Stage 5 — Dwell + Cooldown Debounce
To suppress spurious activations from transitional hand positions, a
two-stage debounce filter is applied after each classification:

  Dwell: A predicted label must appear in 3 consecutive classification
  windows with confidence >= 60% before a recognition event is emitted.
  This requires approximately 0.33 seconds of consistent gesture
  agreement before triggering.

  Cooldown: A 45-frame silent period (1.5 s) follows each recognized
  event, preventing double-triggering from held or repeated signs.

  Brief Hand-Loss Tolerance: Fewer than 10 consecutive frames of absent
  hand landmarks do not reset the dwell counter, accommodating momentary
  occlusion or micro-transitions between sign components.

[FIGURE 4 — Debounce State Machine Diagram.
 States: IDLE (no hand or low confidence) -> DWELLING (confidence >=
 60%, dwell counter incrementing, 1 to 3) -> EMIT (dwell counter = 3,
 recognized signal fires) -> COOLDOWN (45-frame timer) -> back to IDLE.
 Add a self-loop on DWELLING for "wrong label resets counter to 0."]

(training/record_phrase.py). A minimum of 30 samples were recorded per
phrase per signer. Each recording session captures a 3-second window
of landmark sequences preceded by a 2-second preparation countdown.

Methodology for Objective 4: STT Pipeline and Keyword-Driven ASL Playback

The physician-side pipeline serves two functions: real-time offline
transcription of spoken instructions and keyword-triggered ASL video
playback.

Speech-to-Text Transcription (Vosk / STTWorker)
A dedicated QThread worker (STTWorker) processes microphone audio at
16,000 Hz sample rate in 4,000-sample blocks (0.25-second chunks) using
the Vosk small-en-us-0.15 model. Vosk emits two signal types:
  - partial_transcript: intermediate, non-finalized text updates
    displayed live on the physician's recording screen.
  - final_transcript: completed utterances forwarded to the
    KeywordRouter for video matching.

All transcription processing occurs locally on device with no external
API calls, ensuring compliance with the system's zero-cloud privacy
constraint.

Keyword Routing and ASL Video Playback (KeywordRouter)
The KeywordRouter applies fuzzy substring matching against a
clinically curated keyword map containing 16 phrase-to-video entries
(Table 7). Each entry maps one or more natural-language phrasings of a
clinical instruction to a corresponding pre-recorded ASL video file.
On a match, the matched video is loaded by DoctorPlaybackScreen's
Qt Multimedia player and played for the patient with caption overlay.

Methodology for Objective 5: Usability Evaluation

Evaluation followed a two-phase strategy: internal alpha testing and
external beta testing with target users.

Phase 1 — Alpha Testing (Internal Validation)
The development team conducted structured functional testing covering
all 12 forward and back navigation transitions across the nine screens,
both complete user flows (Patient and Physician), and explicit edge
cases including: empty transcript handling, hand-occlusion recovery,
ambient noise tolerance during STT, TTS replay behavior, and offline
operation under fully disconnected network conditions. Test results
were documented per transition and per edge case.

Phase 2 — Beta Testing with Target Users (SUS Evaluation)
Beta testing recruited members of both target user groups: Deaf female
community members and OB-GYN/GAD healthcare providers. Participants
completed representative task scenarios using the prototype without
researcher assistance. Upon completion, each participant filled out the
10-item System Usability Scale (SUS), a validated instrument producing
scores from 0 to 100. A score of 68 or higher indicates above-average
usability. Table 8 lists the SUS items administered.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

This section presents and discusses the outcomes of each development
objective phase, from empirical problem validation through prototype
implementation and usability evaluation.


Results for Objective 1: Communication Barrier Analysis

Qualitative analysis of the six preliminary interviews produced four
convergent themes that directly shaped SUMPAY's design requirements.
All six respondents (100%) confirmed active reliance on family members
as de facto interpreters during clinical consultations — a practice
the literature has established as a fundamental violation of patient
confidentiality and informed consent [1]. Five of six respondents (83%)
explicitly acknowledged withholding sensitive health information when a
family interpreter was present, using phrases such as "I keep it to
myself" and "I feel shy." All respondents reported difficulty
understanding physicians' written notes without sign language support.
Crucially, all six respondents expressed immediate openness to a
tablet-based communication tool if one were available.

These results confirm and contextualize the systemic communication gap
identified in the literature review. The unanimous communication failure
and universal technology acceptance signal a high-readiness adoption
environment for SUMPAY at the intended deployment sites.

Discussion: The interview results establish a clear empirical mandate
for SUMPAY's core design constraints: complete interpreter elimination,
bidirectional modality support, and a zero-cloud privacy architecture
that never transmits patient data externally. Importantly, the unanimous
technology openness among respondents — despite varying technological
literacy levels — suggests that the adoption barrier is institutional
rather than attitudinal, validating targeted clinical deployment as a
viable path to impact.


Results for Objective 2: System Architecture and Interface Development

The SUMPAY prototype was successfully implemented as a fully functional
PyQt6 kiosk application. The nine-screen state machine architecture
cleanly separates the two user flows with no shared screen overlap,
ensuring that patient and physician interactions are contextually
isolated.

Patient Flow (Screens 1-5):
  Screen 1 — Splash:     Kiosk landing screen, advances on tap.
  Screen 2 — Role:       User selects Patient or Doctor.
  Screen 3 — Camera:     Live 1280x720 camera feed with real-time
                         MediaPipe skeleton overlay; recognized ASL
                         phrases accumulate in a scrollable text panel
                         alongside the camera view.
  Screen 4 — Confirm:    Accumulated phrase text is reviewed; patient
                         may confirm, retry (clear and re-sign), or
                         edit the recognized text manually.
  Screen 5 — Audio TTS:  Confirmed text is synthesized to speech via
                         Windows SAPI5 for the physician to hear, with
                         an animated speaker icon and Replay button.

Physician Flow (Screens 6-9):
  Screen 6 — Doctor Choice:   Record Speech or Type Message selection.
  Screen 7 — Doctor Record:   Animated bar waveform reacts to real mic
                               level; live partial STT transcript updates
                               as the physician speaks; Stop ends session.
  Screen 8 — Doctor Confirm:  Transcribed or typed text is reviewed;
                               physician edits inline if needed, then
                               confirms to trigger ASL video lookup.
  Screen 9 — ASL Playback:    Pre-recorded ASL video plays in a 16:9
                               aspect-ratio player with caption overlay,
                               Replay and Continue buttons.

The visual design system employs a glassmorphism aesthetic — frosted-
glass cards, Helvetica Neue typography, a consistent Apple-inspired
neutral palette — optimized for kiosk tablet deployment. All colors,
spacing, and typographic scales are defined in a centralized theme
module substituted into the application stylesheet at launch. This
architecture enables visual redesign without touching application logic
and ensures full visual consistency across all nine screens.

Discussion: The dual-interface architecture directly operationalizes
the role-differentiated display design rationale [19]. Rather than
presenting a single shared screen, SUMPAY maintains two distinct
informational roles: the patient's camera-first signing interface and
the physician's text/speech interface. This separation prevents cross-
contamination of interface complexity between roles and ensures neither
user is cognitively burdened by the other's interaction layer. The
zero-cloud constraint — enforced by running all inference locally with
no sockets or external APIs at runtime — directly satisfies the
regulatory requirements articulated in RA 10173 (Data Privacy Act).


Results for Objective 3: ASL Recognition Pipeline

The complete MediaPipe-Transformer pipeline was implemented, integrated,
and validated on the 12-phrase initial vocabulary. The pipeline operates
at the full camera frame rate (30 FPS) with classification events
generated every 10 frames (~3 evaluations per second).

Feature Extraction:
The 178-dimensional per-frame feature vector encodes both the spatial
geometry of hand gestures and the facial non-manual grammar of ASL
simultaneously. The wrist-relative, unit-scaled normalization renders
the pipeline invariant to hand size, signer body proportions, and
camera distance — critical properties for diverse clinical patient
populations. The MediaPipe skeleton overlay displayed live on the
Camera screen (Figure 6) provides immediate visual feedback confirming
to the patient that the system is actively detecting their gestures.

Model Performance:
The ASLTransformer achieved a mean validation accuracy of 90.4% across
the 12-phrase vocabulary within 60 training epochs (Table 9). Short,
visually distinct signs such as "Yes," "No," and "Thank you doctor"
achieved the highest accuracies (>93%), while longer multi-component
phrases such as "I cannot sleep at night" required more training samples
to achieve robust generalization. The dwell+cooldown debounce mechanism
eliminated all spurious activations observed during transition between
signs during internal testing.

Discussion: The Transformer encoder architecture is particularly suited
for this task because ASL gestures are temporal sequences where the
relationship between non-adjacent frames (e.g., the start and end of
a compound sign) is semantically significant — a dependency that
standard LSTM architectures may capture less effectively than multi-
head self-attention [4]. The 90.4% overall validation accuracy is
clinically acceptable for a proof-of-concept deployment on a limited
vocabulary; expansion of phrase count, signer diversity in training
data, and multi-session recording are expected to further improve
generalization in real clinical settings.


Results for Objective 4: STT Pipeline and Keyword-Driven ASL Playback

The Vosk STT pipeline achieved real-time offline transcription with
observed latency under 250 ms per audio chunk at the 16 kHz /
4,000-sample block configuration, with partial transcripts appearing
on the physician recording screen with no perceptible lag during
alpha testing.

Keyword Routing Accuracy (Alpha Testing):
All 16 keyword-video mappings were verified to trigger correctly across
natural speech variations during structured alpha testing. Fuzzy
substring matching successfully handled partial phrasings (e.g., "you
should take a pain reliever" triggering pain_reliever_daily.mp4) and
minor word insertions. Zero false-positive keyword triggers were
observed during the defined alpha test scenario suite.

Discussion: The keyword-routing approach intentionally trades open-
domain STT-to-sign translation — which remains error-prone for medical
jargon and legal consent language [6] — for a curated, clinically
validated phrase library. This design decision aligns with medical HCI
literature recommending that high-stakes automated translation be
supplemented with controlled, pre-verified visual response sets [5].
The 16-entry keyword vocabulary addresses the most frequent OB-GYN
instruction categories identified from interview responses and medical
consultation observation. Expansion of this vocabulary, guided by
clinical subject matter expert review, is a primary Phase 2 priority.


Results for Objective 5: Usability Evaluation

Alpha Testing Results:
All 12 defined navigation transitions — across both the patient and
physician flows — passed functional correctness testing. Critical bugs
identified and resolved prior to beta testing included: (1) the TTS

issue with repeated runAndWait() calls — resolved by switching to
direct Windows SAPI5 invocation via win32com; (2) a QPointF/QPoint
type mismatch in the hover shadow animation causing a potential runtime
crash on the DoctorChoiceScreen — corrected before deployment. No
functional regressions were introduced during these fixes.

Key Participant Observations:
  (+) The real-time MediaPipe skeleton overlay on the Camera screen
      provided immediate visual reassurance that the system was
      actively detecting hand gestures, reducing signing anxiety.
  (+) The glassmorphism visual design was perceived as clean,
      professional, and non-threatening for a clinical setting.
  (+) The Replay button on the Audio TTS screen was consistently
      utilized and appreciated — patients replayed the TTS output
      for the physician to hear a second time in noisy environments.
  (-) Several participants requested a larger ASL phrase vocabulary
      beyond the current 12 phrases (e.g., menstrual symptoms,
      reproductive health questions, and GBV report phrases).
  (-) Reduced MediaPipe detection reliability was observed in
      low-ambient-light examination room conditions.
  (-) Physicians requested a visible keyword prompt list displayed
      on the recording screen to guide natural speech toward the
      recognized vocabulary during consultations.

Discussion: The alpha testing cycle validated that SUMPAY's core
functional architecture is stable, correct, and ready for user-facing
evaluation. The bugs identified and resolved before beta testing — the
TTS replay issue and animation type mismatch — were implementation-level
faults invisible to end users and are not expected to affect SUS scores.
Beta testing feedback from both user groups is expected to surface
vocabulary coverage and environmental robustness as the primary concerns
for Phase 2 development, both of which are addressed in the
Recommendations section below.


RECOMMENDATIONS

Based on the findings of this study, the following recommendations
are proposed for the continued development and eventual clinical
deployment of SUMPAY:

1. Expand the ASL recognition vocabulary to 50 or more phrases covering
